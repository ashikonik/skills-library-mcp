# Hooks and Titles

Patterns for opening lines and title options, and the failure modes to avoid in
each.

---

## Hooks

A hook is a **promise**. It has to make the rest of the piece worth staying
for. If the hook promises something the piece does not deliver, it is worse
than no hook — it is a refund request.

### Patterns that work

| Pattern | Shape | Example |
|---|---|---|
| The outcome, first | "After three weeks, [result]." | "After three weeks of rewriting one landing page, it converted 40% better." |
| The costly mistake | "I [did X] and it cost [Y]." | "We shipped on Friday and it took the site down for six hours." |
| The contrarian claim | "[Common practice] is wrong, and here's why." | "Most landing pages have too much copy. Here's the proof." |
| The specific number | "[Number] [surprising thing]." | "Nine out of ten startup decks open with the market size." |
| The unfinished scene | Start in the middle of something happening. | "—and then the whole room went quiet." |
| The stake | Name what's at risk before anything else. | "If this migration fails, we lose a quarter of the customer base." |

### Failure modes

- **The greeting.** "Hey guys, welcome back to the channel." Cuts to nothing.
- **The roadmap.** "Today we're going to cover three things." The viewer does
  not need the agenda; they need the first thing to be interesting.
- **The credential.** "As a founder with 10 years of experience…" Trust is
  established by the content, not the introduction.
- **The rhetorical question.** "Ever wondered why your videos don't get
  views?" It is answered by the video, so it wastes the first five seconds
  proving nothing.
- **The definition.** Starting with what a term means. Nobody watching already
  doesn't know it; people who do know it have left.
- **The apology.** "Sorry for the long update." Nobody asked, and it spends
  the attention you were trying to buy.
- **"In this video."** See "The greeting" and "The roadmap".

### A note on sound-off viewing

For vertical and social formats, the opening frame carries the hook when sound
is off — a caption, a striking visual, motion. The spoken hook and the on-screen
hook must say the same thing, not different things. If they say different
things, the viewer gets two half-messages and neither lands.

## Titles

A title is a promise the thumbnail does not have to keep. It should be
**specific and incomplete**: enough to be interesting, not enough to spoil.

### Patterns that work

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| Specific number | "Seven Rules I Broke for Two Years" |
| Negative space | "The Part Nobody Tells You About [Topic]" |
| Outcome | "How [Specific Change] Cut [Specific Number]" |
| Tension | "We Deleted [Thing]. It Made [Result]." |
| Direct but not flat | "[Task] in [Time], Without [Common Obstacle]" |

### Failure modes

- **The label.** "Introduction to [Topic]", "[Topic] Tutorial", "[Topic] (Part
  1)". It describes the file, not the content, and it is what the search
  results already said.
- **The restatement.** The title repeats the hook or the thumbnail's sentence.
  Two devices saying one thing is one device wasted.
- **The superlative.** "The Ultimate Guide", "Everything You Need to Know".
  Both are claims that the piece then has to disprove.
- **The keyword stuffing.** "How to Edit a Video | Video Editing Tips for
  Beginners". It reads as a filing label, and the audience for it is people
  who already know how to search.
- **The false claim.** "The only way to X" or "Nobody talks about this".
  It converts on the click and refunds on the watch.

### Offering options

When you offer titles, offer **three, on different axes** — not three
paraphrases:

1. The one that leads with the outcome.
2. The one that leads with the tension or the mistake.
3. The one that leads with the specificity (a number, a name, a constraint).

Paraphrases of one idea waste the choice you were offering. If the user asks
for "ten titles", the first three should be on different axes and the rest can
explore them.
